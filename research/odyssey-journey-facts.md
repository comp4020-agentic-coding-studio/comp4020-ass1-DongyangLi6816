## Coverage statement

This sheet enumerates **18 stops/episodes** in Odysseus's own telling and the
frame narrative that surrounds it, from departure out of Troy to the sleeping
arrival on Ithaca (Books 9–13, framed by Books 5–8 and the start of 13). This
is a full enumeration of the stops named in the poem for that stretch, not a
selection — every item below is listed with its book reference; none were
dropped for narrative appeal. The count of "18" is a grouping choice, not a
fact the poem states as a number: splitting the two Aeolus visits, the two
Charybdis passages, or the Underworld's many individual shades into separate
items would change it. That grouping choice is marked explicitly at each item
where it applies.

Two adjacent stretches of the poem are **out of scope** for the main list
because they are not Odysseus's own journey: the Telemachy (Books 1–4, his
son's search for news of him) and the reclaiming of Ithaca after arrival
(Books 13–24, no further travel). Both are recorded as appendices below for
completeness, since they are part of "getting home" in the broader sense even
though they are not stops on the sea voyage.

## Narrative structure (fact, not interpretation)

The poem does not narrate the voyage in chronological order. Books 1–4 (the
Telemachy) and Book 5 onward (Odysseus on Calypso's island, then among the
Phaeacians) are told in the poem's own present tense. Books 9–12 — the
"Apologoi" — are a flashback: Odysseus narrates them himself, at the
Phaeacian court, on the night before he is sent home. Chronologically, the
events of Books 9–12 happened *before* Book 5; the poem opens in medias res
near the end of the wanderings (Calypso's island) and only afterward goes
back to tell the earlier stops. The enumeration below is given in
chronological (story) order, not the poem's narrated (telling) order.

## Per-stop facts

### 1–2. Departure from Troy; Ismarus, the Cicones (Book 9)

- Odysseus's fleet of **12 ships** sails from Troy and lands at Ismarus, city
  of the Cicones.
- The men "sack the town," kill the men, and divide the plundered wives and
  goods evenly among the crews.
- Odysseus wants to leave immediately; the men refuse and stay on the shore
  drinking wine and killing sheep and oxen.
- Surviving Cicones summon reinforcements from inland, described as more
  numerous and skilled in chariot and infantry warfare, arriving "as thick as
  leaves and bloom in summer."
- The Achaeans hold their lines through the morning but are overwhelmed by
  evening and retreat to the ships.
- Losses: **"half a dozen men from every ship"** (Butler translation) — i.e.
  six men per ship, twelve ships. (A secondary source, cited on the Cicones
  Wikipedia page, gives "more than seventy men total"; 6 × 12 = 72, consistent
  with that figure.)
- Odysseus performs funeral rites, invoking each fallen comrade's name three
  times, before sailing on.

### 3. Storm off Cape Malea (Book 9)

- Rounding Cape Malea, a north wind drives the fleet off course for **nine
  days**.
- They reach the land of the Lotus-Eaters on the tenth day.

### 4. The Lotus-Eaters (Book 9)

- Odysseus sends a scouting party of **three men** to investigate the
  inhabitants.
- The Lotus-Eaters do the scouts no harm but give them the lotus fruit to eat.
- The lotus is described as causing the eater to "leave off caring about
  home" and want only to stay and eat lotus with the Lotus-Eaters — a
  narcotic, "peaceful apathy" effect.
- All three scouts succumb.
- Odysseus forces the weeping men back to the ships, binds them "fast under
  the benches," and immediately sets sail so no one else tastes the plant.
- Later classical writers place the location in North Africa: Herodotus near
  the Gindanes on the Libyan coast; Polybius and Strabo identify it with the
  island of Djerba (ancient Meninx), off Tunisia. (Location identifications
  are later classical scholarship, not the Homeric text itself.)

### 5. The Cyclopes — Polyphemus (Book 9)

- The fleet reaches an island of wild goats near the land of the Cyclopes;
  Odysseus takes **one ship and its crew** to explore the mainland, leaving
  the rest of the fleet at the goat island.
- They enter a cave belonging to Polyphemus, a one-eyed Cyclops, son of
  Poseidon, containing pens of sheep and goats and stores of cheese.
- Polyphemus returns, blocks the cave entrance with a massive stone, and,
  ignoring the customs of guest-hospitality, eats men in three separate
  meals:
  - First evening: seizes and eats **two** men.
  - Next morning: eats **two more** before leaving with his flocks.
  - Following evening: eats **two more** on his return.
  - Total eaten: **six men**.
- Odysseus gives Polyphemus strong, undiluted wine (from Maron), filling the
  bowl **three times**, drained three times.
- Asked his name, Odysseus answers **"Noman"** (Outis).
- While Polyphemus lies in a drunken stupor, Odysseus and **four chosen men**
  heat a stake of green olive wood (cut to about six feet, one end sharpened
  and fire-hardened) and drive it into his single eye, twisting it "as though
  boring a hole in a ship's plank with an auger."
- Polyphemus's cries of "Noman is killing me by fraud! Noman is killing me by
  force!" bring other Cyclopes to the cave entrance, who conclude he is only
  sick or being punished by a god, and leave without helping.
- Next morning, the blinded Polyphemus lets his flock out to graze, feeling
  only the backs of the animals passing. Odysseus ties the rams together in
  threes with willow withies, hiding one man under the belly of the middle
  ram in each group; Odysseus himself clings under the belly of the largest
  ram.
- Once aboard ship and offshore, Odysseus shouts his true name and lineage
  back at Polyphemus, against his men's pleading.
- Polyphemus prays to his father Poseidon for revenge: that Odysseus either
  never reach home, or reach it "late and in sore plight after losing all his
  men," in another man's ship, and find trouble waiting at home. The text
  states Poseidon hears this prayer.
- Polyphemus then hurls two massive rocks at the departing ship, both narrowly
  missing.

### 6. Aeolia — Aeolus and the bag of winds (Book 10)

- Aeolus, keeper of the winds by appointment of Zeus, hosts Odysseus for a
  month.
- On departure, Aeolus gives Odysseus an ox-hide bag containing all the
  contrary winds, bound with a silver thread "so tightly that not even a
  breath of a side-wind could blow," and lets only the favorable West Wind
  blow to carry the ships toward home.
- Odysseus personally holds the rudder for **nine days and nights** without
  sleeping. On the tenth day, land comes into view close enough that the
  crew can see "the stubble fires burning" — identified in retellings as
  Ithaca.
- Odysseus falls asleep. His crew, believing the bag holds hidden gold and
  silver from Troy, open it.
- The released winds raise a storm that blows the fleet back to Aeolus's
  island.
- Odysseus returns as a suppliant asking for renewed help. Aeolus refuses
  angrily, declaring Odysseus "abhorred of heaven" and ordering him off the
  island: "him whom heaven hates will I in no wise help."

### 7. The Laestrygonians (Book 10)

- The fleet finds a landlocked harbor surrounded by steep cliffs with a
  single narrow entrance. **Eleven of the twelve ships** enter and moor
  together inside; Odysseus keeps his own ship moored outside, at a rock near
  the entrance.
- Odysseus sends **two men and a herald** to scout. They meet the daughter of
  Antiphates, a Laestrygonian king; at his house they find his wife is
  gigantic, and Antiphates immediately seizes and eats one scout.
- The other two flee; giant Laestrygonians pour out and hurl huge rocks down
  from the cliffs onto the trapped ships, smashing them, then spear the men
  in the water "like fish."
- Only Odysseus's own ship, moored outside the harbor, escapes by cutting its
  cable and rowing hard.
- Losses: **11 of 12 ships and their crews destroyed**; the poem does not
  give a specific head count for the men lost.
- The single surviving ship sails on to Aeaea, the island of Circe.

### 8. Aeaea — Circe (Book 10)

- Odysseus scouts alone, sees smoke from a house, and returns to the ship
  rather than investigating further himself.
- He divides the remaining crew into two companies (his own, and one under
  Eurylochus) and draws lots; Eurylochus's company of **twenty-two men** is
  chosen to investigate.
- Approaching Circe's house, they encounter tamed mountain wolves and lions
  that Circe has drugged into submission; the animals fawn on the men rather
  than attacking.
- Circe invites the men in and gives them cheese, honey, meal, and Pramnian
  wine, secretly drugged to erase memory of home. After they drink, she
  strikes them with her wand and turns them into pigs, though their minds
  remain human.
- Eurylochus alone suspects treachery, does not enter, and returns to warn
  Odysseus.
- Odysseus sets out alone to rescue them. Hermes (sent by Athena, per the
  Wikipedia summary) intercepts him on the way, gives him the herb **moly**
  as protection against Circe's magic, and instructs him to threaten her with
  his sword when she attempts to strike him, and to make her swear an oath by
  the gods not to harm him further before accepting her invitation to bed.
- Odysseus follows the instructions; Circe, unable to affect him, restores
  the transformed men to human form.
- The crew remains on Aeaea for **one year**.
- Before departure, Circe tells Odysseus he must first travel to the
  Underworld to consult the dead — a journey the text describes as one no
  mortal has made before — and provides the means to do so.
- **Elpenor's death** (during this stay, told at the start of Book 11):
  Elpenor, described as the youngest of the crew, gets drunk and sleeps on
  the roof of Circe's house. Woken by the commotion of departure, he forgets
  he is on the roof, falls, and breaks his neck, dying instantly. His absence
  goes unnoticed in the haste to leave; his body is left unburied on Aeaea.

### 9. The Nekyia — journey to the Underworld (Book 11)

- Odysseus sails to the edge of Oceanus and the land of the Cimmerians to
  perform the rites Circe prescribed, calling up the shades of the dead.
- Shades encountered or named, in the order the text presents them:
  1. **Elpenor** — the first shade to meet him, before the burial rites owed
     him have been performed; asks Odysseus to return to Aeaea and give him
     a proper cremation and burial.
  2. **Tiresias** — Theban prophet; delivers his prophecy of the homecoming
     and warns against harming the cattle of Helios on Thrinacia.
  3. **Anticleia** — Odysseus's mother, daughter of Autolycus.
  - Catalogue of heroines (women he sees at the pool of blood):
  4. **Tyro** — daughter of Salmoneus, seduced by Poseidon disguised as the
     river Enipeus.
  5. **Antiope** — daughter of Asopus, bore Amphion and Zethus to Zeus.
  6. **Alcmena** — wife of Amphitryon, bore Heracles to Zeus.
  7. **Megara** — daughter of Creon, wife of Heracles.
  8. **Epicaste** (Jocasta) — mother and wife of Oedipus.
  9. **Chloris** — wife of Neleus, mother of Nestor, Chromius, Periclymenus,
     and Pero.
  10. **Leda** — wife of Tyndareus, mother of Castor and Pollux.
  11. **Iphimedeia** — wife of Aloeus, mother of Otus and Ephialtes.
  12. **Phaedra**
  13. **Procris**
  14. **Ariadne** — daughter of Minos.
  15. **Maera**
  16. **Clymene**
  17. **Eriphyle** — described as one who "sold her own husband for gold."
  - Heroes of Troy:
  18. **Agamemnon** — describes his murder by Aegisthus and Clytemnestra on
      his return home.
  19. **Achilles** — appears together with Patroclus, Antilochus, and Ajax.
  20. **Patroclus**
  21. **Antilochus**
  22. **Ajax**, son of Telamon — still resentful over the dispute for
      Achilles's armor; refuses to speak to Odysseus.
  - Judges and sinners in Hades:
  23. **Minos** — son of Zeus, judging the dead with a golden sceptre.
  24. **Orion** — giant hunter, driving the ghosts of beasts he killed in
      life.
  25. **Tityos** — son of Gaia, punished for violating Leto.
  26. **Tantalus** — punished with eternal, unreachable hunger and thirst.
  27. **Sisyphus** — condemned to roll his stone eternally.
  28. **Heracles** — his phantom only; the text states the "real" Heracles
      feasts with the immortal gods.
  - Mentioned but not actually seen: Odysseus says he would have liked to see
    **Theseus and Pirithous**, but fled (in fear of the Gorgon's head, per the
    poem) before they could appear.

### 10. Return to Aeaea (Book 12, opening)

- Odysseus returns to Aeaea as promised, cremates Elpenor's body, buries his
  ashes with his armor, and marks the grave with an oar from the ship.
- Circe gives further directions for the route home and warnings about what
  lies ahead: the Sirens, then a choice of routes past Scylla and Charybdis
  or the Wandering Rocks, and the danger of Thrinacia.

### 11. The Sirens (Book 12)

- Circe warns that anyone who hears the Sirens' song and stops will never be
  welcomed home by wife and children again, and describes "a great heap of
  dead men's bones" lying around the Sirens' field as evidence of past
  victims.
- The Butler translation refers to "the two Sirens" (grammatical dual in the
  original Greek indicates exactly two, though later artistic tradition often
  depicts three).
- Odysseus melts a wheel of wax, softens it in his hands and the sun, and
  stops his men's ears with it.
- He has himself bound upright to a crosspiece partway up the mast, with
  instructions to the crew to bind him tighter, not looser, if he begs to be
  released.
- The ship passes safely; no losses.

### 12. Scylla and Charybdis — first passage (Book 12)

- Scylla is described as having twelve misshapen feet and six necks, each
  ending in a head with three rows of teeth set close together.
- Charybdis is a whirlpool that draws in and vomits out the sea three times a
  day; Circe states even Poseidon could not save a ship caught in its suction.
- Circe's advice: hug the Scylla side rather than risk Charybdis, since
  losing a few men is preferable to losing the whole ship and crew.
- During the passage, Scylla "shoots out all her heads at once" and seizes
  **six** men, described as Odysseus's best men — snatched from the ship as
  it passes.

### 13. Thrinacia — the Cattle of Helios (Book 12)

- The island holds seven herds of cattle and seven flocks of sheep, fifty
  head in each flock, tended by Helios's daughters Phaethusa and Lampetia.
- Both Tiresias (in the Underworld) and Circe warned in advance that harming
  the cattle would doom the ship and crew, though Odysseus himself might
  survive alone.
- Adverse winds sent by Poseidon becalm the ship on the island for **one
  month**.
- Provisions run out. Eurylochus persuades the starving crew that a quick
  death at sea is preferable to slow starvation, and leads them to kill the
  best of the cattle while Odysseus is away praying, first offering prayers
  with oak leaves in place of barley-meal (none being available).
- Omens follow the slaughter: the hides are said to crawl, and the roasting
  meat makes a sound like the lowing of live cattle.
- The crew feasts on the cattle for **six days**.
- On the seventh day, favorable winds return and they sail. Helios, informed
  by his daughter, demands recompense from Zeus, threatening to shine his
  light in the underworld among the dead instead of the sky. Zeus agrees to
  destroy the ship.
- Once at sea, Zeus strikes the ship with a thunderbolt; the ship is wrecked
  and **all the men drown except Odysseus**.

### 14. Second passage past Charybdis (Book 12)

- Odysseus lashes the mast and keel together to form a makeshift raft and
  drifts; the wind shifts and carries him back toward Charybdis.
- As Charybdis sucks the raft down, Odysseus leaps up and clings to an
  overhanging fig tree, "like a bat," unable to find footing since the roots
  are too far below and the branches too high to grasp with the raft.
- He waits until the raft resurfaces, drops back onto it, and rows away by
  hand.
- Scylla does not see him this time.
- He drifts for **nine days** and reaches Calypso's island, Ogygia, on the
  tenth.

### 15. Ogygia — Calypso (Book 5, referenced elsewhere)

- Calypso, a nymph, holds Odysseus on the island of Ogygia for **seven
  years**.
- She wants him to remain permanently as her husband and offers him
  immortality.
- Odysseus spends his days sitting on the shore weeping, longing for home,
  though the text describes him sleeping with her at night "against his
  will."
- Athena appeals to Zeus for Odysseus's release. Zeus sends Hermes to order
  Calypso to free him.
- Calypso reacts with anger, protesting that the gods begrudge goddesses
  their mortal lovers (citing the precedents of Orion and Iasion), but
  complies, since she cannot defy Zeus.

### 16. Departure from Ogygia; Poseidon's storm (Book 5)

- Calypso gives Odysseus a bronze axe with an olive-wood handle and an adze
  to build a raft, and supplies bread, wine, water, and clothing.
- He cuts **twenty trees** total (alder, poplar, and pine), and builds a
  deck, gunwale, mast, yard-arm, rudder, and wicker hurdles against waves;
  Calypso supplies linen for the sail.
- Construction takes **four days**; he departs on the fifth.
- He sails, navigating by the stars (the Pleiades, Boötes, and the Bear kept
  to his left, per Calypso's instructions), for **seventeen days**, sighting
  the mountains of Phaeacia on the eighteenth.
- Poseidon, spotting him from the mountains of the Solymi, is angered that
  the other gods let him escape. He raises a storm with winds from all four
  directions, sweeps Odysseus overboard, breaks the mast, and tears away the
  sail.
- Odysseus swims back to the raft; a further massive wave then shatters the
  raft completely.
- Ino (Leucothea), a sea goddess who was formerly the mortal daughter of
  Cadmus, takes pity on him, appears rising from the waves, and gives him an
  enchanted veil, instructing him to strip off his clothes, abandon the raft,
  bind the veil round his chest, and swim for shore, then return the veil to
  the sea once ashore (which he does).

### 17. Scheria — the Phaeacians (Books 6, 7, 8, 13 opening)

- Odysseus swims ashore and hides among bushes near a river mouth.
- Athena, disguised as a sea-captain's daughter, appears to Princess Nausicaa
  in a dream, prompting her to go wash clothes at the seashore the next day.
- Nausicaa and her maids play ball on the beach; their shouts wake Odysseus.
  He emerges naked but for foliage; the maids flee, but Nausicaa, "encouraged
  by Athena," stands her ground, gives him clothing, food, and drink, and
  directs him to her father's palace.
- Athena cloaks Odysseus in mist for protection as he enters the palace,
  since the Phaeacians are wary of strangers. He supplicates Queen Arete by
  embracing her knees. The elder Echeneus urges King Alcinous to extend
  hospitality, and Alcinous does.
- The palace is described with silver-and-gold guard dogs made by Hephaestus,
  bronze walls, golden gates, an evergreen orchard, and golden statues that
  serve as torch-bearers.
- Odysseus competes in athletic games held by the Phaeacians.
- The bard Demodocus sings of the Trojan War; Odysseus weeps, prompting
  Alcinous to ask his identity.
- Odysseus reveals his name and recounts the full story of his wanderings
  (Books 9–12) to the assembled court.
- The Phaeacians give him gifts: each noble contributes, at Alcinous's
  urging, "a large tripod and a cauldron" beyond the clothes and gold already
  given, funded by a general levy so no single citizen bears the cost alone.
  Alcinous personally checks that the goods are stowed securely on the ship
  so nothing breaks loose and injures the rowers.

### 18. Voyage home asleep; arrival at Ithaca (Book 13)

- After a farewell feast, Odysseus boards the Phaeacian ship and lies down on
  a rug and linen sheet; as the crew rows out, he falls into a deep,
  "almost deathlike" slumber.
- The Phaeacian ships are described as needing no pilots or rudders, knowing
  all destinations by thought, and sailing safely even in fog; Alcinous
  recalls one once carrying Rhadamanthus to Euboea and back in a single day.
  The ship carrying Odysseus is described as swifter than a falcon.
- Poseidon, angry that the Phaeacians escorted his enemy home, complains to
  Zeus. Zeus advises him to turn the returning ship to stone in full view of
  the Phaeacian city. Poseidon does so, striking it with the flat of his hand
  and rooting it to the sea floor — fulfilling an old prophecy, recalled by
  Alcinous, that Poseidon would one day wreck a returning ship and "bury
  their city under the mountain."
- Odysseus wakes on the shore of Ithaca without recognizing it, since Athena
  has shrouded the land in mist to keep his return secret. He is left asleep
  on the sand near a large olive tree and a sacred cave of the sea god
  Phorcys — a cave with stone bowls and looms, and two entrances, one for
  mortals and one reserved for gods.
- Finding his goods (the Phaeacian gifts) intact, Odysseus is met by Athena,
  disguised as a young shepherd carrying a javelin. He tells her a false
  story about fleeing Crete after a killing; she reveals her true form,
  praises his cunning, and helps him hide his treasure inside the cave,
  sealing the entrance with a stone.
- Athena warns him of the danger from Penelope's suitors, directs him to seek
  out his swineherd, and disguises him as an old beggar — withering his
  skin, removing his hair, blearing his eyes, and dressing him in rags with a
  torn wallet and a staff — making him unrecognizable to everyone, including
  his own family.

## Appendix A — the Telemachy (Books 1–4)

Out of scope for the main list: this is Telemachus's journey, not Odysseus's.
Recorded for completeness only.

- Odysseus has not returned ten years after the fall of Troy (twenty years
  since he left Ithaca for the war). Penelope's palace is occupied by many
  suitors (a commonly cited secondary-source figure is 108) pressing her to
  remarry.
- Penelope delays remarriage for three years by claiming she must first weave
  a burial shroud for Laertes, weaving by day and secretly unweaving it each
  night; a servant eventually betrays the trick to the suitors.
- Athena, disguised first as "Mentes" and then as "Mentor," urges Telemachus
  to seek news of his father.
- Telemachus visits Nestor at Pylos, then Menelaus and Helen at Sparta, who
  reveal that Odysseus is held captive by Calypso.

## Appendix B — reclaiming Ithaca (Books 13–24)

Out of scope for the main list: no further travel occurs here; this is the
resolution after arrival. Recorded for completeness only.

- Disguised as a beggar, Odysseus is sheltered unknowingly by his loyal
  swineherd, Eumaeus.
- Telemachus returns from Sparta and is reunited with his father, who reveals
  his identity to him alone.
- Odysseus and Telemachus hide the suitors' weapons in the palace.
- Odysseus's old dog, Argos, recognizes him and dies immediately after.
- The old nurse Eurycleia recognizes Odysseus by a scar on his leg while
  washing his feet.
- Penelope sets an archery contest with Odysseus's own bow as a condition for
  remarriage; Odysseus, still disguised, is the only one able to string and
  shoot it.
- Odysseus and Telemachus kill the suitors; Telemachus hangs the female
  servants who had consorted with them.
- Penelope tests Odysseus's identity by asking about their bed, built around
  a living, rooted olive tree and immovable — a detail only the real
  Odysseus would know; he answers correctly and they are reunited.
- Odysseus reveals himself to his father, Laertes.
- The suitors' families gather to avenge the killings; Zeus and Athena
  intervene to halt the feud and establish peace.
