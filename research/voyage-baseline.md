# How long a smooth voyage home would take — fact sheet

Research note, not part of the deployed site. Facts only; no interpretation,
theme, or design decisions are recorded here.

Companion to `homeric-returns.md`. That sheet records what the poem *states*
about other captains' returns. This one records the **modern-scholarship
baseline** — distance and sailing speed — and every inference drawn from it.

**Everything in this sheet that is not a recorded ancient passage time is
INFERRED and labelled as such, with the arithmetic shown.** The evidential
status here is weaker than in the primary-text sheets, deliberately kept
separate for that reason.

## Sources consulted

- Lionel Casson, *Ships and Seamanship in the Ancient World*, ch. 12
  (pp. 270–296). Pagination is identical between the 1971 Princeton and 1995
  Johns Hopkins editions, so a page cite works for either.
- Lionel Casson, *Ships and Seafaring in Ancient Times* (1994), p. 67 — the
  *Olympias* trireme reconstruction.
- Hal Roth, *We Followed Odysseus* — the Troy→Ithaca distance, from a sailor who
  retraced the route.
- Julian Whitewright, *IJNA* 40.1 (2011) — independent re-run of Casson's method.
- Gal, Saaroni & Cvikel, *IJNA* (2023) — contests Casson's windward figure.

## The headline number

**A fleet leaving Troy could expect to be home in about a week.**

Two independent anchors converge on this:

### Anchor 1 — the primary text

Diomedes reached Argos **on the fourth day** (Bk 3). Stated, not computed. See
`homeric-returns.md` for the quote and the Butler/Greek divergence.

### Anchor 2 — a recorded ancient passage departing from Troy

Casson's fleet table (p. 293) records: **Troy → Alexandria, 550 nautical miles,
7 days, 3.0 knots.** Troy→Ithaca is 565 nmi — 2.7% farther.

> 7 days × 565/550 = **7.2 days — INFERRED**

The only inference is a 2.7% scaling of a recorded passage time. This is a far
stronger basis than distance ÷ assumed speed, and it is the number to use.

## Casson's speed figures (page-verified)

| Condition | Speed | Page |
|---|---|---|
| Merchantman, favourable wind | 4–6 knots ("slightly less while working through islands or along coasts") | p. 288 |
| Merchantman, head wind | under 2–2½ knots made good | p. 291 |
| **Fleet, favourable wind** | **2–3 knots** | p. 296 |
| **Fleet, unfavourable or light** | **1–1½ knots** | p. 296 |

⚠️ **Casson's knots are distance ÷ whole elapsed days — a 24-hour clock.** They
already absorb overnight stops. Dividing again by a 12-hour sailing day
subtracts the night twice. (This error was made and caught during research; the
corrected fleet-band range is 7.8–11.8 days, not 15.7–23.5.)

Corroboration: Whitewright (2011) independently re-ran Casson's method on 20
voyages — favourable average **4.4 kn** (range 3.0–6.2), unfavourable average
**1.8 kn**.

## The distance

**565 nautical miles ≈ 1,046 km. MODERN MEASUREMENT, not an ancient figure.**
No ancient stated distance for Troy→Ithaca was found in any source checked.

Derivation: Hal Roth's figure, from *We Followed Odysseus* — he retraced the
route in a 35-foot sloop from Troy near the Dardanelles mouth to Ithaca.

Independent check: a 27-waypoint great-circle sum over a coastal/island-hopping
route (Troy → Lesbos → Psyra → Chios → Samos → Ikaria → Cyclades → Milos → Cape
Malea → Cape Tainaron → Pylos → Zakynthos → Ithaca) gives **1,067 km = 576 nmi,
within +2.0% of Roth**. A coarser 15-waypoint version gives 534 nmi. Straight-line
great circle is 274 nmi but crosses land — useless except as a floor.

Rounding the Peloponnese is obligatory; no Corinth Canal until 1893.

**Use ~565–576 nmi (~1,046–1,068 km).**

## The Bronze Age caveat — load-bearing

**No Bronze-Age or Homeric voyage appears anywhere in Casson's tables.** His
entire speed dataset is Greco-Roman recorded passages, roughly 1st c. BC – 5th
c. AD (Pliny, Diodorus, Procopius, Acts).

Homeric-era craft were an *eikosoros* or *triakontoros* — c. 20 m, under 10
tonnes, an oared galley with an auxiliary square sail, beached nightly — not a
round-hulled Roman *corbita*.

**"4–6 knots with a fair wind" is a Roman merchantman number. Applying it to
Odysseus's ships is an extrapolation and must be labelled as one.** What
transfers with real confidence is the nightly-beaching practice and the daylight
coasting day. The speed does not.

Casson is explicit about the gap, p. 279 n. 37:

> "We have no information on how fast an ancient galley could spurt."

He accepts only that "better than 7" knots was probably the ceiling, and at
p. 128 adds "and that for no very long time." He refuses the famous 8-knot
Aemilius Paulus dash as fleet evidence (p. 292). The measured *Olympias*
reconstruction gives 8 knots sprinting and **4 knots for hours** with half the
oarsmen rowing in shifts (1994, p. 67) — and is acknowledged **incapable** of
cruising all day at 7–8 knots. The widely-quoted "triremes cruised at 7–8 knots"
is a claim about ancient authors, not a measurement.

## Night sailing — splits by ship type

- Merchantmen on open crossings **did** run through the night (p. 290 n. 87,
  p. 293).
- Coastal and Aegean island-hopping traffic did not: vessels "frequently put in
  at a convenient port for the night," and Aegean traffic "often laid over at
  whatever island they reached by evening" (p. 292).
- Oared galleys are unambiguous: they "put in at shore each night to allow the
  crews to forage for their dinner" (p. 90), and "certainly never voluntarily
  spent a night on the water" (p. 103).

**The primary text corroborates this directly** — Book 13, the Phaeacian ship
arriving at Ithaca: "She had so much way upon her that she ran half her own
length on to the shore." These hulls were beached, not moored.

Casson allows about **15 hours a day** under sail for coasting (p. 291 n. 93),
if a daylight-only variant is wanted.

## The ancient day's run — two independent sources converge

- Herodotus, 700 stades by day = 124.3 km = **67.1 nmi**
- Menippus, 700 stades for an ordinary vessel, which Casson glosses as 70 nmi
  (p. 288 n. 81). ⚠️ Casson's own hedge, which should be kept: "whether he means
  24 hours or just the daylight hours is not certain."
- Aristides, from personal experience, 1,200 stades ≈ 120 nmi day-and-night.
- Arnaud on ancient distance-reckoning: ancient sea distances were **time-based
  units** (1,000 stadia = a day and a night), implying ~4 knots and 50–100 nmi
  per day.

Ancient testimony brackets **70–120 nmi/day → 4.7–8.1 days** for this route.

## Gaps — leave empty

- **No beam-reach or quartering figure exists in Casson.** His taxonomy is
  strictly favourable vs unfavourable. Do not fill this.
- **Casson's 1951 *TAPA* article was not obtained** (JSTOR blocked, Internet
  Archive lending-restricted, HathiTrust 403). No knot figures from it are
  verified. **Cite the book, not the article.**
- **Casson's windward figure is contested** — Gal, Saaroni & Cvikel (2023) argue
  the ~2-knot upwind number is physically implausible at his seven-point limit.
  Cite it alongside, not instead.
- **ORBIS cannot be cited for a sea speed** — the v1.0 paper contains zero
  occurrences of "knot" and defers its parameters elsewhere.
